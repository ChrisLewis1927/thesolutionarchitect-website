Assessing the current state

Map what exists, find the risks and constraints, and use evidence to shape what comes next.

You'll come away with:

  • How to build a proportionate view of the current state.
  • How to identify technical debt and legacy risks that matter to the work.
  • How to assess integrations and dependencies.
  • How to separate symptoms, assumptions and reported information from verified evidence.

Why current state assessment matters

A future-state design has to work with the environment around it. Existing systems, data, processes, contracts, skills and dependencies can all constrain the options available or affect how difficult they are to deliver.

This is particularly important during discovery. Before choosing technology, you need enough understanding of the existing landscape to know what the service depends on, what can change, what cannot easily change and where the important unknowns remain.

The depth of the assessment should match the work. A small change to an existing service may only require a focused view of the affected components and dependencies. A major replacement or transformation may need a much broader baseline. The aim is to understand enough of the current state to make the next decisions well, rather than document the entire organisation by default.

A system list is only the starting point. The relationships between systems, data, processes, people and suppliers are often where the important constraints appear.

Assess the parts of the current state that could change the options, risks or transition approach.

Mapping existing systems

Start with the information that already exists: architecture diagrams, service catalogues, configuration records, repositories, support documentation and supplier material. Treat these as evidence to check rather than unquestioned truth or something to dismiss automatically.

Compare documentation with what is actually running. Depending on the scope, that might mean checking configuration, monitoring, logs, deployment pipelines and support records, or walking through the service with the people who use and operate it. When sources disagree, record the discrepancy rather than quietly choosing the version that looks most plausible.

For systems that matter to the work, understand enough to explain their purpose, users, ownership, important data, upstream and downstream dependencies, support arrangements and lifecycle position. Capture manual steps too. A spreadsheet export, scheduled file transfer or rekeying process can be an important service dependency even though it is not represented as a formal integration.

Do not expand the exercise without a reason. If a system cannot affect the problem, proposed options, delivery path or risk, documenting it in detail may add little value.

Understanding technical debt

Technical debt and legacy technology can overlap, but they are not interchangeable. An older system can remain supported and suitable for its purpose, while a relatively new system can already contain significant technical debt. What matters is the effect on the service and the decisions you need to make.

Look for debt or legacy characteristics that materially affect areas such as security, supportability, reliability, recovery, integration, ability to change, operating cost or access to the skills needed to maintain the service.

Concentrate on consequences rather than labels. Instead of recording that a component has "bad technical debt", record the evidence and what it means: a dependency is no longer supported, deployments require a manual process known by one person, a database version restricts an upgrade, or changing an interface requires supplier involvement.

You do not need a complete catalogue of every compromise ever made. Prioritise the items that could influence the solution, increase delivery or operational risk, or require explicit treatment during transition.

Assessing integration points

Integrations deserve particular attention because they create dependencies between systems and teams. A proposed change may be technically straightforward inside one system but difficult once upstream consumers, downstream services, data contracts or supplier constraints are considered.

For each material integration, establish the information needed to judge its importance and risk. Depending on the situation, that can include:

  • what the integration is for and which systems depend on it
  • what data is exchanged and any relevant sensitivity
  • how often data moves and what volumes or latency matter
  • the interface, protocol or transfer mechanism
  • authentication and other relevant security controls
  • what happens when the integration fails and how it recovers
  • who supports it and who can approve or make changes
  • supplier, licensing, contractual or change-window constraints

Use evidence where it exists. API specifications, job configuration, monitoring, incident records and operational logs usually tell you more about behaviour than a line on an old diagram.

Lack of documentation is a useful warning sign, but it does not tell you the level of risk on its own. Criticality, failure behaviour, recoverability, security, support and ease of change all matter.

Documenting the current state honestly

Current-state documentation should distinguish between what has been verified and what the team has been told, inferred or has not yet established.

A useful approach is to make the status clear in the material itself. A finding might be a verified fact, a reported behaviour, an assumption that still needs testing or an open question. Record important sources and dates where they help someone understand how confident to be in the information.

Avoid judgement disguised as analysis. Terms such as "poor", "brittle", "obsolete" or "unmaintainable" need evidence behind them. "The supplier ended support in March" or "deployments currently depend on a manual step performed by one team" tells the reader what is actually wrong and gives them something they can act on.

This is especially important when different teams have different views of the same system. Architecture work should make those differences visible and help establish the evidence, rather than decide which team has the better story.

If an important finding cannot yet be verified, record the uncertainty rather than turning it into a fact.

Capability mapping

A system view may still be too narrow when the problem involves how the organisation actually delivers an outcome. Capability mapping can help you look beyond individual applications and consider the people, processes, information and technology involved.

For example, the capability to process grant applications is not provided by a grant system alone. It also depends on the people doing the work, the assessment process, the information they need and the surrounding services they rely on.

This helps when evaluating change. Replacing the technology may remove one constraint while leaving the process, skills or information problem untouched. It can also reveal similar capabilities being delivered in different parts of the organisation, although similarity alone is not evidence that they should be consolidated.

If you use a maturity model, define the criteria and the evidence behind each assessment. A maturity score is only useful if people understand what it measures and how it affects the decisions being made.

Pain points versus symptoms

People experiencing the current service are an important source of evidence. When they say a system is slow, information is hard to find or a process repeatedly fails, the problem is real even if the underlying cause is not yet understood.

Treat the reported pain as the starting point for investigation. A slow service might involve inefficient queries, insufficient capacity, network delay, a dependency performing badly or a workload that peaks at particular times. More than one factor may contribute.

Test those possibilities using the evidence available: monitoring, traces, query timings, capacity data, incident records, support tickets, user research and observation of the process. The cause may also sit outside the technology, such as a manual approval, duplicated data entry or a policy rule being implemented inefficiently.

The important distinction is between what people experience and what the evidence shows is causing or contributing to it. Designing around an assumed cause risks solving the wrong problem.

Documentation that changes decisions

The output should make the implications of the current state easy to find. Depending on the scope, that might include a landscape or context diagram, important data flows and integrations, constraints, material technical debt, operational risks, assumptions and unresolved questions.

Put the findings that affect decisions first. Explain which constraints narrow the options, which existing assets are worth retaining or reusing, which dependencies need protecting, which risks need mitigation and which unknowns need further investigation before a decision can be made.

Keep the supporting evidence behind those conclusions so another person can understand how they were reached. The current-state view is a snapshot, so date it and revisit the relevant parts when the environment changes or new evidence invalidates an assumption.

The assessment has done its job when it changes the reasoning that follows: the requirements you refine, the options you consider, the risks you manage or the transition you plan.

A useful current-state assessment makes the next architecture decision better informed.

This is a practitioner guide to assessing the current state for solution architecture in UK central government, not official guidance. The depth and approach vary by organisation, service and the decision in front of you.
