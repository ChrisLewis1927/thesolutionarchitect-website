A department needs a team to take a service through discovery. Another has developers but needs a specialist to work through a difficult integration. A council wants a delivery partner to help modernise several services while building the skills to maintain them afterwards.

Each needs digital capability, but the requirements are different. Buying a discovery is a different proposition from bringing an architect into an existing team or appointing a partner for a programme of work.

Digital Outcomes and Specialists 7 provides a procurement route for all three. Before using it, you need to be clear about which kind of support you are buying.

#### What DOS7 is

[Digital Outcomes and Specialists 7](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1043.9), usually shortened to DOS7, is a framework agreement run by the Government Commercial Agency (GCA), which was Crown Commercial Service until April 2026. Its agreement number is RM1043.9. It combines the previous Digital Outcomes 6 and Digital Specialists and Programmes frameworks.

Eligible public sector organisations can use it to buy digital, data and technology services from suppliers appointed to the agreement. The scope includes agile development, user-centred design and specialist skills, with work spanning discovery, alpha, beta, live and retirement.

The framework establishes the supplier pool, contractual terms and buying rules in advance. A buyer then runs a competition for its requirement and awards an individual contract under the framework. That contract is called a call-off.

This saves buyers from establishing the whole commercial arrangement themselves. There is still plenty of work to do locally: defining the requirement, evaluating suppliers and agreeing how delivery will be managed.

#### Four lots, four different needs

DOS7 is divided into four lots, described in the [framework specification](https://assets.gca.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/RM1043.9-Framework-Schedule-1-Specification-v1.0-1.docx). They can sound similar until you put an actual requirement against them.

| Lot | What you buy | Example need | Maximum call-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1: Digital Outcomes | A supplier delivering a defined digital project using agile methods | A supplier to deliver a discovery | 48 months |
| 2: Digital Capability and Delivery Partners | Longer-term transformation, capability building and strategic resource augmentation | A partner to support several services over time and develop your internal capability | 72 months |
| 3: Digital Specialists | Individual specialists with particular digital skills for defined work | A solution architect to join your existing team for a defined piece of work | 24 months |
| 4: User Research Studios and Participants | Research space, and participants with the characteristics needed to test a service | Participants with particular accessibility needs | 36 months |

The maximum terms include extensions and come from the [buyer guide](https://assets.gca.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/RM1043.9-DOS-7-Buyer-Guide-v1.4.odt).

The lot describes the kind of support available. Responsibility for delivery is agreed separately, for each statement of work. The specification sets out three accountability models:

* **Sole Responsibility.** The supplier accepts the full risk of delivering the agreed milestones.
* **Self-Directed Teams.** The supplier provides teams that produce increments commissioned by the buyer, and carries some of the risk.
* **Rainbow Teams.** Supplier staff work alongside the buyer’s own, and may be directed day to day by someone outside the supplier.

These correspond to the Outsource, Borrow and Bridge delivery models in the Digital, Data and Technology Playbook. The choice has a tax consequence too. The specification notes that it is likely to influence IR35 status, and the buyer is responsible for deciding whether the work is a contracted-out service or a supply of resource.

That is a useful conversation to have before writing a list of roles. “We need six people” tells a supplier about capacity. It says much less about what those people must achieve or what support they will get from the department.

Lot 4 covers a smaller but necessary part of delivery. Recruiting suitable research participants takes work, especially when a service needs to reach people with particular experiences or accessibility needs. Testing with colleagues who already know the process can miss problems that are obvious to someone using it for the first time.

#### What changed with version 7

DOS7 opened in January 2026 and operates under the Procurement Act 2023. It uses an open framework model, consisting of successive frameworks that allow the supplier pool to be refreshed.

GCA describes a six-year arrangement reopening every 18 months. The [buyer guide](https://assets.gca.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/RM1043.9-DOS-7-Buyer-Guide-v1.4.odt) gives the overall agreement an end date of 29 January 2032. The agreement page lists the current framework to 29 July 2027, and it is due to reopen in 2027 as [DOS 7.2](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1043.9.2).

There are contractual changes too. GCA’s [comparison with DOS6](https://assets.gca.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/RM1043.9-key-changes-DOS6-to-DOS7-v1.0-1.odt) identifies revised award procedures and changes to documents covering intellectual property, security and sustainability.

Anyone who has used DOS6 will recognise much of the approach. The documents still need reading afresh, particularly where an earlier procurement is being used as the starting point for a new one.

#### How buying works

DOS7 requires further competition. There is no option to award without competition, and each procurement is awarded to a single supplier.

Buyers define their requirements and assessment criteria, invite eligible suppliers to respond, evaluate the responses and complete the call-off contract. They can use a single-stage, two-stage or multi-stage competitive selection process. [GCA’s buying guidance](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1043.9) sets out the process.

The agreement page directs buyers to publish requirements through the Contract Award Service, accessed through the Public Procurement Gateway. The buyer guide also allows buyers to use their own procurement platform after completing “Search and Save” in the filtering tool. Supplier filtering is mandatory for Lot 3 and optional for Lots 1, 2 and 4.

A useful specification gives suppliers enough information to understand what they are taking on. That includes the awkward parts: a legacy integration with little documentation, an approval that another team controls, or restrictions on access to production data. If those details are left out, suppliers bid on less information and the uncertainty has to be dealt with after award.

There is room for learning within an agile requirement. A discovery should be able to change the organisation’s understanding of a problem. Buyers can still explain the questions they need answered, the constraints on the work and the evidence they expect at the end.

#### Where G-Cloud fits alongside it

G-Cloud and DOS7 often appear in the same conversation because both are routes for buying public sector technology. G-Cloud has [its own post](what-g-cloud-is-and-why-so-much-public-sector-technology-is-bought-through-it.html).

[G-Cloud 15](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1557.15) covers cloud services and cloud support. DOS7 covers digital outcomes, delivery capability, specialists and research facilities. GCA specifically directs buyers needing bespoke design and development from G-Cloud towards DOS7.

DOS7 excludes standalone hosting, support and maintenance of a live application or website. Its [agreement page](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1043.9) lists the exclusions and points buyers towards other agreements.

A service may therefore involve several related procurements. Buying the team that designs and builds it does not necessarily cover the infrastructure it runs on or the support it needs afterwards. Those requirements should be considered together when planning delivery, even where they use different commercial routes.

#### What matters for an architect

Architects can help make the requirement understandable before suppliers submit their proposals.

Start with the environment. Which systems must the service integrate with? Which platforms are already available? What data will it handle? What recovery expectations apply? Which decisions can the delivery team make, and which need agreement elsewhere?

Then consider what the organisation needs to receive. A demonstration may show that a feature works, but the team taking ownership will also need to understand the design, deployment process, dependencies and operational behaviour.

The specification includes knowledge transfer requirements and explicitly addresses building internal capability under Lot 2. In a procurement, it helps to say what that should achieve. Can departmental staff deploy a change, investigate a failure and explain the important design decisions when the supplier leaves?

Price needs similar care. The buyer guide explains the available pricing mechanisms and how supplier day rates can be checked against framework maximums. Comparing day rates is useful, but it will not tell you the whole cost of delivering and running a service. Integration effort, internal staff time, operational support and eventual exit all belong in that discussion.

A supplier’s place on DOS7 gives it access to the competition. The buyer still has to assess the proposed team and approach against the work. Before the tender goes out, someone needs to work through who will make decisions, how the supplier will work with existing teams and what the department must be able to do when the contract ends.
