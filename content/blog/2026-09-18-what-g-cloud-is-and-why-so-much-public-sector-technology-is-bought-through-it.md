A council needs a new case management system. A government department needs somewhere to host a digital service. A university wants help moving off an ageing data centre. Every one of them is spending public money, and public money comes with procurement rules. Running a full standalone procurement for every cloud service would take time and effort from commercial, technical and legal teams.

G-Cloud makes that process simpler. It establishes the suppliers, commercial terms and buying process in advance, giving public sector organisations a quicker route to buying cloud services.

The [Government Commercial Agency](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/g-cloud-relaunches-with-biggest-upgrade-in-its-history) estimates that around £3 billion of public sector cloud spend will flow through G-Cloud each year.

#### **A framework, not a shop**

G-Cloud is a framework agreement. Suppliers are evaluated for a place on the framework, agree to its commercial terms and publish information about their services and pricing. Buyers can then select from that supplier pool using the framework's call-off rules.

That makes G-Cloud different from simply browsing an online shop. The framework establishes the contractual route, but the buyer still has to define what it needs, assess suitable services and follow the correct award process. G-Cloud is run by the Government Commercial Agency (GCA), which was created in April 2026 when Crown Commercial Service became part of the new organisation.

The current agreement is [G-Cloud 15, RM1557.15](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1557.15).

Earlier G-Cloud versions operated as separate framework agreements. G-Cloud 15 changes that model: it is an open framework, made up of successive frameworks that allow the supplier pool to be refreshed during its four-year life.

#### **Why it was created**

G-Cloud first launched in February 2012. At the time, government wanted to reduce its dependence on large, long-term technology contracts and make it easier for smaller suppliers to sell services to the public sector. The original [CloudStore announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/cloudstore-opens-for-business) described short contracts, initially no longer than 12 months, as a way of making it easier for buyers to change supplier and understand the cost of exit.

That approach also supported a wider shift towards cloud services. The government's current [Cloud First policy ](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/government-cloud-first-policy)says public sector organisations should default to public cloud first and use other approaches where public cloud cannot meet the requirement. The policy is mandatory for central government and strongly recommended for the wider public sector.

G-Cloud is one of the main procurement routes available for putting that policy into practice. It has also retained its focus on widening supplier access. According to GCA, approximately 90% of suppliers on G-Cloud 15 are small and medium-sized enterprises.

#### **What you can buy**

G-Cloud 15 is divided into five lots.

1. **Lot 1a: Cloud infrastructure and platform services** - This covers Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) and Platform as a Service (PaaS): services such as compute, storage, networking and managed platforms used to build and run systems.
2. **Lot 1b: Cloud infrastructure and platform services above OFFICIAL** - This covers similar infrastructure and platform services for data classified above OFFICIAL, where additional security requirements apply.
3. **Lot 2a: Cloud infrastructure software** - This covers software used to operate, secure or manage infrastructure, delivered as a cloud service.
4. **Lot 2b: Software as a Service** - This covers ready-made applications delivered over the cloud, where the customer subscribes to software rather than building and hosting it themselves.
5. **Lot 3: Cloud support** - This covers cloud-specific professional services for a defined period, such as migration, implementation, configuration and support.

What unites them is that they are defined cloud services offered through the framework, with published service information and pricing.

G-Cloud is not intended for hiring a team to design and build a bespoke digital service. GCA directs buyers with that kind of requirement towards [Digital Outcomes and Specialists 7.](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1043.9)

#### [](https://www.gca.gov.uk/agreements/RM1043.9)**What changed with G-Cloud 15**

G Cloud 15 opened for public sector use in August 2026. GCA calls it the biggest upgrade in the framework's history, and the changes are substantial.

It brings Cloud Compute 2 (previously a separate framework) together with the G Cloud 14 lots into a single agreement. It is valued at around £14 billion over a four year term. Call off contracts can now run for up to 60 months, against 36 months plus an optional 12 month extension under G Cloud 14. GCA also describes the supplier assessment as its most thorough yet, evaluating each supplier on quality, technical ability and price.

It is also the first G Cloud to run under the [Procurement Act 2023](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/contents), which came into force on 24 February 2025. That makes it an open framework, meaning it reopens to new suppliers during its life. There is an 18 month reopening window after launch, and further reopening at set points across the four years, so new and growing businesses are not locked out until the next version.

#### **How buying works**

A wide range of public sector organisations can use G-Cloud, including central government departments, local authorities, NHS and health bodies, education organisations, emergency services, devolved administrations and other eligible public bodies.

G-Cloud 15 provides two main award routes:

* For straightforward, relatively low-value or low-complexity requirements, a buyer may be able to award without competition. The buyer filters available services against its requirements and checks which services meet them. Where the conditions for this route are satisfied, the award is made to the lowest-priced matching service after the required checks have been completed.
* For more complex, higher-value or tailored requirements, the buyer can run a competitive selection process. The buyer defines its requirements and assessment criteria, invites eligible suppliers to submit tenders and evaluates them to identify the most advantageous tender.

Both routes are important because being listed on G-Cloud does not mean every supplier or service is suitable for every requirement. Buyers use GCA's digital services to identify and shortlist suitable offerings and then complete the applicable award process and call-off documentation.

The framework makes procurement easier, but it does not remove the buyer's responsibilities. The organisation still needs to define the requirement properly, keep an audit trail of its decision and follow its own commercial, financial, security and architecture governance.

#### **What G-Cloud does not do**

Being appointed to G-Cloud means a supplier has passed the assessment required to join the framework. It does not mean every service it offers is automatically suitable or secure for every public sector organisation, the buyer still has to assess the service against its own requirements. That includes understanding what data will be processed, where responsibility sits between customer and supplier, what security controls are provided and how the service will be configured.

The [National Cyber Security Centre's Cloud Security Principles](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/cloud/the-cloud-security-principles) provide a framework for assessing cloud services. The NCSC is clear that cloud customers remain responsible for deciding whether a service meets their security requirements and for configuring it appropriately. Data protection responsibilities do not disappear either. Using a cloud supplier does not remove an organisation's responsibilities under UK data protection law.

#### **Price also needs some care**

G-Cloud services publish pricing information, but a published price does not automatically make a service the best value for a particular requirement. Buyers still need to compare services against what they actually need, including whole-life cost, contract terms, implementation effort, support and exit.

Fourteen years on, the idea behind G-Cloud still holds: establish the commercial rules and supplier pool centrally, publish services and pricing, then give public bodies a quicker route to choosing and contracting for the cloud service they need.
