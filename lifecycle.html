Architecture across the delivery phases

Government services move through discovery, alpha, beta and live, and one day they are retired. Anything a phase leaves unanswered becomes the next phase's problem.

You'll come away with:

  • What the Service Manual expects in each phase, and where service assessments actually sit
  • Where the architect's attention belongs in discovery, alpha, beta and live
  • What the April 2026 assurance changes mean for moving between phases
  • How to plan retirement so a service can be switched off without stranding anyone

Government services move through discovery, alpha, beta and live, and one day they are retired. The architecture question changes at every step, and anything a phase leaves unanswered becomes the next phase's problem.

Four phases, three assessments and an exit

The GOV.UK Service Manual describes four phases: discovery, alpha, beta and live. Beta has two stages, private and public. Retiring a service has its own guidance, separate from the phases. Discovery and alpha each end in a decision about whether to carry on, and the manual is clear that stopping is a legitimate answer.

Three service assessments sit along the way. The alpha assessment comes before the move into beta. The beta assessment is the step from private beta into public beta, and the live assessment comes at the end of public beta. Discovery has none.

Not every service has to be assessed. The Service Manual says a transactional service for central government must be, even one used only by civil servants, with an exception for low volume forms built on an assured form building platform. A cross government panel takes services likely to handle more than 100,000 transactions a year, or used by civil servants in more than one organisation. Departmental panels take the rest. A green result means the service can continue to the next phase. Amber means it can continue while the team fixes the issues, usually within three months. Red means it stays where it is until the critical issues are fixed and reassessed. Departmental panels may rate a service met or not met instead.

The approvals around the phases changed in 2026. The Digital Assurance Playbook removed digital and technology spend controls from 1 April 2026. Approving a business case to move from one phase to the next now sits with the organisation. GDS is involved only where the spend exceeds the organisation's Delegated Authority Limit or is judged novel, contentious or repercussive, and those cases also need HM Treasury approval. The playbook still asks assurers to check that a new service is aligned with the principles in the Service Manual, including through the service assessment process. Some Service Manual pages still mention spend controls approval; that reflects the process before April 2026.

The Service Standard itself is moving. In July 2026 GDS said it is reimagining the Service Standard as part of a shift away from a static, assessment led model. It described the work as at an early stage and gave no dates, so check the current guidance before relying on the assessment details here.

Discovery: learn the constraints before anyone designs

Some of the work starts before discovery does. The Digital Assurance Playbook says assurance must be undertaken before any procurement or discovery phase activities. Initiatives over the reporting threshold, £5 million whole life cost for anything other than cryptographic products, go on the pipeline at the earliest sensible time, unless your organisation sets a different threshold. Your organisation also sets its own checkpoints for when.

Discovery itself is about understanding the problem before committing to build. The Service Manual asks teams to learn about their users and what they are trying to achieve, the constraints on changing how the service runs, the policy intent behind the work and the chances to improve things. It says you should not start building the service in discovery. There is no set length, though four to eight weeks is typical.

The constraints are where an architect earns a place. The manual lists legislation, contracts, legacy technology and existing processes and systems, and asks the team to separate the hard constraints, such as primary legislation, from soft ones like an existing process, which it says to change rather than work around. Its guidance on choosing technology fills in the technical side: the systems and data sources the service may need to work with and whether they are changing, why earlier technology decisions were made, where to find existing components and what the security threats are.

"Nothing gets built in discovery, so it doesn't need an architect."

Nothing should get built, and the Service Manual's team guidance does not list an architect among the discovery roles. It gives teams access to a technical architect from alpha. But legacy technology and existing systems are on its own list of discovery constraints, and somebody has to read the estate to judge which of them are hard. A hard constraint nobody spotted in discovery comes back in alpha as a surprise.

Discovery is finished when the team has decided whether to move on to alpha, weighing whether there is a viable service to build and whether it is cost effective to pursue. The manual says it is not a failure to stop there, and it names alternatives to building anything: publishing content, running a campaign, or making data or an API available to others. If the honest architectural answer is that the existing system can be changed, or that no new system is needed, discovery is the place to say so.

Discovery is the cheapest point at which to say the answer is not a new system.

Alpha: test the riskiest assumptions

Alpha is where the team tries out different solutions to the problems found in discovery, by building prototypes and testing ideas. The Service Manual is direct about what those prototypes are for. Build things just complex enough to test ideas, not production quality code, and expect to throw the code away at the end. You do not have to prototype the whole journey, and it often makes sense to focus on the parts you expect to be hardest. Alpha services should not be open to the public, and alphas tend to last six to eight weeks.

Some of the riskiest assumptions are technical. The manual's own example is Register to vote, whose team realised it would need an API that integrated with the registration systems of more than 400 local councils. If that had not been possible the service would not have worked, so that is what the alpha focused on. Integration, data reuse and legacy constraints are the assumptions an architect is best placed to test, and alpha is the last cheap moment to find out one of them is wrong.

At alpha, the manual expects you to show you have started thinking about:

  • the programming tools you would choose for beta, and why they would be value for money
  • how you will identify threats to the service, deal with them and keep up to date with them
  • how you will open source your code
  • whether you will use common platforms
  • how users would be affected if the online part of the service had technical problems

It also says to start planning monitoring in alpha, agreeing what to monitor, how, and how issues will be recorded. The choosing technology guidance says you must follow the Technology Code of Practice when choosing technology, and adds a warning worth heeding: it is easy to get the boundaries between components wrong early on, so use prototypes to learn about them and be ready to change your mind.

Constraints get sharper too. By the end of alpha you should be able to explain how the service will meet user needs within the immovable constraints, and where a constraint could be removed over time, what the organisation plans to do about it. The code is disposable. The decisions it informed are not, so record the significant ones as you make them.

Throw the alpha code away. Keep the reasons.

To move on to beta the team needs to be confident it can create something that meets users' needs and is cost effective, with the budget and people to deliver it. If it cannot say that, the manual's options are to stop, or to repeat discovery or alpha.

Beta: build it for real, then open it up

Beta takes the best idea from alpha and builds it for real. The Service Manual frames it as thinking about how the service will integrate with or start to replace existing services, and preparing for live. In private beta a limited number of invited people use the service so the team can learn and improve it. Once the team is confident it can run the service at scale, it takes a beta assessment to move into public beta, which opens the service to anyone who needs it.

Replacement is where beta gets architecturally hard. The manual says to keep a legacy service running until the new service moves into its live phase. Throughout beta, then, two services do overlapping jobs. Decide which one is the system of record for each set of data, how users move from one to the other, and what happens if the new service has to be pulled back.

Several things become firm at the step into public beta. The monitoring guidance says you must have monitoring in place by then. Accessibility audit issues have to be fixed before the move, and an accessibility page published when it happens. If the service reuses information that users have already given another part of government, you will need to show it is shared in a way that is secure, stable and works at scale, for example through an API that follows the government API standards.

The beta assessment has no fixed list of questions, but the manual says you will usually need to talk about technology, and its examples include how you deploy software safely and often, keep users' data secure, handle cookies, make source code open, manage the limits of your stack and toolchain, use open standards and common platforms, manage availability and test the service. The panel asks about the decisions you made and how you built the service, so keep the architecture records in step with what is actually running.

Live: run it, improve it, keep it changeable

Live is about supporting the service in a sustainable way while continuing to improve it. The live assessment comes at the end of public beta, and the manual says to spend public beta preparing for live. Running a live service does not necessarily mean keeping an agile team on it all the time; the team should work out during public beta how much continuous improvement to support, and who it needs to do that.

Before a service goes live, the manual says the team needs to be sure it can:

  • secure the service's information
  • maintain uptime and availability, and monitor the status of the service
  • continue vulnerability and penetration testing
  • continue testing service performance and maintain quality assurance

The people who support users also need to understand the online part of the service, which can mean updating call centre scripts or training operations staff. During public beta the team should start a roadmap for the work that continues in live, including the constraints still to be addressed, such as legacy technology.

Security stays live too. For central government departments and arm's length bodies, the Secure by Design principles must be met by delivery teams throughout the service life cycle, and the team guidance expects a live team that can evaluate the security impact of changes, manage security alerts and retire service components securely.

The cost of change needs the same attention. The government's guidance on preventing technical debt and legacy says plans must show how you will prevent the build up of technical debt and future legacy. It asks for a named owner for the service's technical health and money set aside for upgrades. It was published before the April 2026 changes and still describes the old spend control process, but the Digital Assurance Playbook asks assurers to check the same thing: that new work does not introduce future legacy.

Retirement: plan the exit before you need it

Services are retired when policy changes, when evidence shows users' needs have changed, or when so few people need a service that running it is no longer cost effective. The Service Manual's guidance on retiring your service starts where the build did, with the user need. Either the need no longer exists, a new government service will meet it, or government will stop meeting it, and each case changes what the team has to hand on and to whom.

Then there are the people to tell. The GOV.UK team needs to know as soon as retirement is likely. People who use the service directly need to know what is changing, what they will have to do and what will happen to their data. Assisted digital users must be told too, through the service's assisted digital channel and support organisations. API consumers need time to update their software, so the manual says to contact them as soon as possible.

Some of the infrastructure has to outlive the service. After retirement you must keep using SSL and serve a redirect to the GOV.UK start page. If the service was live for less than six months, that lasts until a year after it went live. Otherwise it lasts a year from retirement, or until the current certificate expires if that comes first. The information the service holds needs a plan for keeping it secure, support to maintain the retention policies, and, if it passes to a new service owner, a transfer made in line with data protection policy and explained to users.

An exit is easier when it was designed for. Exit terms in contracts, data that can be exported in a usable form and APIs that can be versioned and deprecated are all choices you can make in alpha and beta. The live team guidance expects a team that can retire service components securely; the architecture has to make that possible.

The cheapest time to plan an exit is before there is anything to exit from.

This guide is one practitioner's account of architecture across the UK government delivery phases, not official guidance. The architect's focus in each phase is a teaching aid; the authoritative requirements are in the Service Manual and the government sources below.
