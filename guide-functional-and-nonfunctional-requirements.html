Functional and non functional requirements

What a system does barely shapes its architecture; how it has to behave shapes almost everything. This guide is about those behavioural demands, the non functional requirements, and why they, not the feature list, decide most of what you build.

You'll come away with:

  • Why non functional requirements drive architecture decisions more than the feature list does.
  • How to pin down performance, scalability, availability and security as targets you can test.
  • Why accessibility and security are designed in from the start, never added at the end.
  • A method for writing requirements that are specific, measurable and verifiable.

Requirements do two different jobs. Functional requirements describe what a service needs to do. Non-functional requirements describe how well it needs to do it and the conditions it has to work within.

As a solution architect, you need both. Functional requirements tell you which capabilities the service needs. Non-functional requirements, usually shortened to NFRs, help you work out which designs can deliver those capabilities at the required level of performance, security, reliability and supportability.

This guide looks at the difference between the two, the main NFRs you are likely to encounter, how to make them measurable, and how to use them when making architecture decisions.

Functional and non-functional requirements

A functional requirement describes something the service must allow a user, another system or a business process to do.

For an online reporting service, functional requirements might include allowing a user to sign in, submit a report, save a draft, amend a submission or receive confirmation that it has been accepted.

An NFR describes a quality or constraint that applies to those functions.

Type Question it answers Example
Functional What must the service do? Allow a business to submit its annual energy report.
Non-functional How well must it do it, and under what conditions? Continue to accept submissions during the expected deadline-day traffic peak.

The distinction is useful, but do not get too hung up on taxonomy. You may also encounter business constraints, technical constraints, policy requirements and standards. A requirement to use an organisation's existing cloud platform, for example, can be treated as an organisational or technical constraint rather than a service-quality NFR.

What matters to the architect is that these requirements are identified early enough to influence the design.

Why NFRs matter to architecture

Imagine a government service that allows businesses to submit an annual energy report.

Its basic functions are straightforward: a user signs in, completes a form, the service validates the information, stores the submission and sends confirmation.

There are many ways you could build that. A conventional web application could provide those functions. So could a serverless design or a set of independently deployed services. The functional requirements alone do not give you enough information to choose between them.

Now add some operational context. Around 50,000 reports are expected in a two-week submission window, with a sharp increase close to the deadline. The service needs to remain available while that deadline is open. It must be accessible. The information is classified OFFICIAL, with some information potentially requiring the -SENSITIVE marking. A small team will support the service, and the reporting rules are likely to change each year.

You still have not selected an architecture, but you now have useful criteria for comparing the options.

A design that is difficult for a small team to operate may be a poor fit. A design that cannot handle the deadline peak needs a different scaling approach. A platform or component that makes accessibility difficult introduces risk that another option may avoid.

This is where NFRs become useful to an architect. They turn general expectations into criteria you can use when comparing designs and explaining trade-offs.

The main NFR areas

There is no universal list that fits every service. The following categories are a useful starting point when you are working through a design.

Area Questions to ask
Performance How quickly does the service need to respond? Which transactions are most sensitive to delay?
Scalability How much demand is expected? Is growth steady, seasonal or concentrated around deadlines?
Availability and resilience When must the service be available? How quickly must it recover? How much data loss is acceptable?
Security and privacy What information and functions need protecting? Who needs access? What threats and legal obligations apply?
Accessibility Who needs to use the service? What accessibility standards apply? How will they be tested?
Operability How will the service be monitored, supported, deployed and recovered when something goes wrong?
Maintainability How often is the service likely to change? How quickly must changes be made safely?
Compliance and data What legislation, government policy, retention rules, audit requirements or information standards apply?

Treat the list as a prompt for discussion rather than a template to fill in mechanically. An internal reporting tool and a national transactional service will not need the same targets.

Turn vague expectations into requirements

The difficult part is rarely recognising that performance or availability matters. The difficult part is defining what "good enough" means for this particular service.

A practical way to make an NFR useful is to capture four things:

  1. the quality you care about
  2. a target
  3. the conditions under which the target applies
  4. a way to verify it

For example, "the service must be fast" gives a delivery team very little to work with.

A requirement such as "95% of submission requests should complete within two seconds while the service is handling the expected peak concurrent load" is much more useful, provided you have evidence for the numbers and a plan to test them.

The figures in examples like this are illustrative. They are not default targets. The right number comes from the service you are designing.

Vague More useful
The service must be fast. Define a response target for an important transaction and state the load at which it must be met.
The service must be highly available. Define when the service must be available, how availability is measured and what recovery time is acceptable.
The service must be secure. Define the security outcomes and controls needed for the information, users and threats involved.
The service must scale. Define expected normal demand, expected peak demand and any growth assumptions.

Performance and demand

Performance covers more than page speed. Depending on the service, you may need to consider response time, transaction throughput, integration latency, batch processing windows or the time taken to complete a long-running process.

Frontend performance is another concern. GDS guidance recommends setting a performance budget for page characteristics such as load time, number of requests and page size. That is different from setting response-time targets for an API or back-end process, so it is useful to capture them separately.

Pay particular attention to the shape of demand. An annual average can hide the period that actually matters.

Some government services have predictable peaks around tax dates, grant deadlines, regulatory returns or policy events. If a large proportion of transactions arrive near a deadline, designing only for average demand can give you the wrong capacity assumption.

Use evidence from the existing service where it is available: transaction volumes, monitoring data, peak-to-average ratios and known busy periods. For a new service, document the assumptions you are making and test them as the service develops.

Availability and resilience

Availability is about whether the service is usable when people need it. The percentage on its own does not tell you enough.

You also need to know the period over which availability is measured, whether the target applies 24 hours a day or only during defined service hours, how planned maintenance is treated and what happens when part of the service fails.

Recovery requirements are useful here. Recovery time objective, or RTO, describes the target time within which a service or capability should be restored after a disruption. Recovery point objective, or RPO, describes the point in time to which data should be recoverable after a disruption and therefore indicates the amount of recent data loss that may be tolerated.

Those values should come from the impact of an outage. A service with a statutory deadline may need a different resilience approach from an internal application that can tolerate periods of unavailability outside working hours.

Security and privacy

Security requirements should be identified while the service is being designed. The government's Secure by Design approach expects cyber security risks to be considered from the start and managed throughout the service lifecycle.

Start with the information and the risks around it. Understand the government security classification, any additional markings, the people and systems that need access, the likely threats and the consequences of compromise.

Those requirements feed into decisions about identity, authorisation, network boundaries, encryption, audit, secrets management and how data is stored or shared.

OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE is worth being precise about. Under the Government Security Classifications Policy, OFFICIAL is one of the three classification tiers. The -SENSITIVE marking is an additional marking that can be applied to certain OFFICIAL information; it is not a separate classification tier. Additional markings indicate where additional protective controls or security behaviours are required.

Privacy requirements need the same early attention. If personal data is involved, the architecture may need to support requirements around data minimisation, retention, access, deletion and audit. Where processing is likely to result in a high risk to people's rights and freedoms, a Data Protection Impact Assessment may be required.

The aim is to understand the actual risks and apply controls that are proportionate to them.

Accessibility

Accessibility can influence technology choices, component design, content management and the formats a service produces.

Public sector websites and public-facing mobile apps are generally required to meet WCAG 2.2 AA and publish an accessibility statement, although the regulations contain exemptions, exclusions for some content and provisions for disproportionate burden.

For an architect, the useful question is what the technical design needs to support. That may include accessible authentication journeys, keyboard operation, screen-reader compatibility, semantic content, accessible form components and an appropriate choice of document format.

A particular technology is not automatically accessible or inaccessible. The important thing is to understand what the service needs and make sure the design allows the team to meet and test those requirements.

Operability and maintainability

Once a service goes live, it still needs to be operated, supported and changed, so the support model belongs in the architecture discussion.

Find out who will run the service, what skills they have, when support is available and how much operational overhead the team can reasonably take on.

Operability requirements can cover monitoring, logging, alerting, deployment, rollback, incident investigation, backup and recovery. Maintainability requirements are concerned with how safely and quickly the service can change.

If policy rules change several times a year, for example, the time and effort needed to implement a policy change may be an important requirement. If releases need to happen frequently, deployment and rollback need to be routine enough to support that.

These requirements can materially affect architecture choices. Splitting a system into many independently deployed services might make some changes easier, while increasing the number of components a small operations team has to monitor and support. A simpler deployment model may be easier to operate but introduce different trade-offs elsewhere.

Where should the numbers come from?

Architects should not invent NFR targets because they sound sensible.

Useful evidence can come from existing service analytics, monitoring data, user research, expected transaction volumes, business deadlines, legislation, security assessments, support arrangements and the impact of the service being unavailable.

Sometimes the evidence does not exist yet. That is normal during discovery and early alpha. In that case, record the value as an assumption, explain where it came from and decide how you are going to test it.

It is also worth recording why a target exists. Six months later, another architect should be able to tell whether a requirement is driven by a user need, a legal obligation, a policy decision, an operational limitation or an assumption that can now be revisited.

Use NFRs to compare architecture options

Once the important requirements are understood, use them as part of your architecture evaluation rather than jumping straight to a preferred technology.

Returning to the energy-reporting example, the requirements might lead to considerations like these:

Requirement What it means for the architecture
Large deadline-driven traffic peak The design needs a credible way to add capacity and handle peak demand.
Small support team Operational complexity and the number of components to support matter when comparing options.
Annual changes to reporting rules Ease of change, testing and release becomes part of the evaluation.
Government information requiring appropriate protection Hosting, identity, access, audit and data controls must satisfy the relevant security requirements.
Accessibility requirements Frontend technology and service components need to support accessible implementation and testing.

Notice that none of these requirements says "use microservices", "use serverless" or "use Kubernetes". Those are possible design choices.

Requirements give you the criteria against which those choices can be assessed.

Plan how each requirement will be verified

A measurable requirement is only useful if the team knows how it will be checked.

Performance and scaling requirements may need load or stress testing. Availability can be measured through production monitoring. Security requirements may be verified through technical testing, threat modelling, configuration review and penetration testing. Accessibility needs a combination of automated and manual testing, including testing with assistive technology.

Thinking about verification while you write the requirement is a useful test of whether it is specific enough. If nobody can explain how they would know that the requirement has been met, it probably needs more work.

NFRs and the Service Standard

In government, several points in the Service Standard overlap directly with the areas covered by NFRs.

  • Point 5 covers making sure everyone can use the service.
  • Point 9 covers security and privacy.
  • Point 11 covers choosing appropriate tools and technology.
  • Point 14 covers operating a reliable service.

If you are developing a transactional service for central government, you must get it assessed unless a stated exception applies. This includes transactional services used internally by civil servants. Services that do not meet the criteria for mandatory assessment can still arrange a voluntary assessment.

The assessment should not be the reason you define NFRs. It is another reason to make sure the decisions behind the service can be explained with evidence.

A practical NFR checklist

Before you settle on an architecture, check that:

  • the important functional capabilities are understood
  • the relevant NFR categories have been considered
  • targets are based on evidence where evidence is available
  • assumptions are clearly identified where evidence is missing
  • the conditions under which each target applies are clear
  • the team knows how each important requirement will be verified
  • business and technical constraints are separated from architecture decisions
  • important trade-offs and the reasons for them are recorded

You do not need every NFR to be final in discovery. They should become more precise as the team learns more about the service.

What you do need is enough evidence to recognise the requirements that could change the design. Those are the ones worth finding early.
