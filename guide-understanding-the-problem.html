Understanding the problem before you design the solution

The quickest way to waste time in solution architecture is to design a good answer to the wrong problem. This guide covers the work that comes before solution design: turning a request into a problem you can investigate, understanding the evidence and constraints around it, and deciding whether you know enough to move forward.

You'll come away with:

  • A way to separate a requested solution from the problem it is meant to address.
  • A practical view of what discovery should establish, and how a Solution Architect contributes.
  • A method for distinguishing evidence, assumptions, constraints and decisions.
  • A proportionate test for whether the team understands enough to explore solutions in alpha.

Start with the problem, not the request

Work often arrives in the language of a solution: "we need a new case management system", "move this to the cloud" or "build us a database". The request may be sensible, and it may contain useful clues, but it is not yet a problem statement. If you accept it without examining the need behind it, you narrow the options before you understand what success requires.

Imagine that a policy team asks for a database so analysts can report on building energy performance. Several different problems could sit behind that request. The data may be spread across systems, difficult to access, inconsistent, incomplete or structured for transactions rather than analysis. A new database might help with some of those problems and duplicate them in another place for others.

Begin by asking what people are trying to achieve, who is affected, what happens today and what evidence shows that the current position is a problem. A useful working structure is:

[Users or affected group] cannot [complete a task or achieve an outcome] because [evidenced cause or obstacle], which results in [measurable or observable impact].

This is a teaching aid, not an official government template. Its value is that it makes gaps visible. If you cannot name the affected people, the impact or the supporting evidence, you have identified something to investigate rather than something to fill with a guess.

When someone hands you a solution, work back to the need it is meant to meet.

Treat the first explanation as a hypothesis

The first description of a problem is neither automatically right nor automatically wrong. It is a starting hypothesis. People usually describe the part they can see: a slow screen, a delayed report, a manual workaround or an expiring contract. Those are genuine observations, but several causes may produce the same symptom.

Ask what evidence would support or challenge each possible explanation. Useful evidence can include user research, service analytics, operational data, process observation, incident records, contract information and technical measurements. Compare sources rather than relying on the loudest account in the room.

A chain of "why" questions can help you move beyond a symptom, but it does not prove that you have found one definitive root cause. Real services often have several interacting causes. Treat the chain as a set of hypotheses to test, and stop when further questioning no longer changes the decision in front of you. Chapter 7 looks more closely at discovery-question techniques.

The aim is not to replace a stakeholder's wording with a more impressive version of your own. It is to reach a shared account of the need that is supported by enough evidence to guide the next step.

Discovery builds the evidence for what happens next

The GOV.UK Service Manual guidance on discovery says teams should understand users and what they are trying to achieve, the constraints on changing the service, the underlying policy intent and opportunities to improve the situation before committing to build. What the team learns should inform whether it is worth moving to alpha.

Discovery is multidisciplinary work. User researchers lead research with users; policy colleagues explain the intended outcomes and legal context; business analysts and service designers help map needs, processes and journeys; delivery, commercial, data, security and operational specialists contribute their evidence. Responsibilities vary between organisations and teams.

The Solution Architect's contribution is to connect that evidence to the technical and organisational landscape. This commonly includes:

  • understanding the existing systems, data, integrations, contracts and operational capabilities that shape the problem
  • identifying technical, security, privacy and delivery assumptions that need testing
  • checking whether an apparent constraint is genuine, who owns it and what evidence supports it
  • exploring whether existing services, components or non-technical changes could meet the need
  • making uncertainty and significant risks visible before they harden into design decisions

The Service Manual says not to start building the service in discovery. That does not mean ignoring technology. Its technology guidance asks teams to understand existing systems and data, security threats, available components and changing technical constraints. Early prototypes can also test hypotheses. The distinction is between learning about feasibility and prematurely committing to a product or architecture.

Discovery effort should be proportionate to the uncertainty, risk, cost and impact of the work. The Service Manual says there is no fixed duration, although four to eight weeks is typical. A well-understood change to a small internal service may need less investigation than a new public service spanning several organisations. The important question is what the team needs to learn, not whether it has produced a standard set of documents.

Separate evidence, assumptions, constraints and decisions

Problem framing becomes unreliable when different kinds of information are mixed together. Keep four categories distinct:

  • Evidence: something observed or supported by a credible source, such as research findings, transaction data, legislation, a signed contract or measured system behaviour.
  • Assumption: something currently treated as true but not yet supported well enough, such as an estimated user volume or a belief that a named integration is essential.
  • Constraint: a boundary that limits credible options, such as legislation, an existing contract, an immovable dependency or the capability of the team that will operate the service.
  • Decision: a choice made by someone with the authority to make it, with reasons, consequences and any conditions recorded.

Do not label a constraint "hard" simply because it appears in the brief. The Service Manual distinguishes constraints that are difficult to change, such as primary legislation, from existing processes that teams may need to challenge. Budgets and dates can be fixed in one context and negotiable in another. Security, privacy and accessibility create obligations, but they do not usually prescribe one technical solution.

For every significant constraint, record its source, scope, owner and effect on the work. For every important assumption, record the evidence needed, who will obtain it and when it must be resolved. This prevents a convenient belief from quietly becoming an architecture decision.

Understand users and policy intent together

The first point of the Service Standard asks teams to develop a deep understanding of users and the problem they need the service to solve. This includes the wider context, not only the moment when someone interacts with a government website. Research, analytics and quick prototypes can all contribute evidence.

User needs sit alongside policy intent. The Service Manual describes policy intent as what government wants to change or achieve through a policy. A statement of policy intent should explain the intended outcome and may also explain what must not change. Where no clear statement exists, the team may need to work with policy colleagues and those who commissioned the work to establish a shared understanding from policy documents, legislation, commitments and other evidence.

For architecture, the useful questions are:

  • What outcome is government trying to achieve?
  • Who is affected, including people who may not use the digital service directly?
  • How will success be measured?
  • Which parts of the approach are required by legislation or an authorised policy decision, and which remain open?
  • What changes in policy, evidence or operating context would cause the problem or scope to be reviewed?

Understanding policy intent does not mean converting every policy idea directly into a feature. The Service Manual guidance on policy intent says teams should still examine whether a largely prescribed solution will achieve the intended outcomes, explore alternatives and explain their benefits and consequences. The architect contributes evidence and recommendations; the final authority depends on the decision, governance and role.

Set a useful boundary around the problem

A problem can be framed too narrowly or too broadly. If you define it around one system or team, you may miss the user's wider journey and move the failure somewhere else. If you try to fix every related policy, process and platform at once, the work becomes too large to learn from or deliver.

The Service Standard asks teams to work towards solving a whole problem for users, including working across organisational boundaries where needed. It also warns against creating a service that tries to do too much and supports making incremental improvements. Those points belong together: understand the whole journey, then choose a manageable scope that improves it without pretending the rest does not exist.

Write down what is in scope, what is outside the current scope, the dependencies that cross the boundary and who owns them. Treat the boundary as a decision that may be revisited when evidence changes. This gives the team a clear place to start without turning today's delivery scope into a permanent definition of the problem.

Know when you understand enough to move forward

Discovery is not complete because every question has an answer. It is complete when the remaining uncertainty is understood well enough for the appropriate decision-makers to choose what happens next. Depending on the evidence, that may be an alpha, further research, a change to policy or process, reuse of something that already exists, or stopping the work.

Before recommending a move into alpha, check whether the team can answer these questions:

  • What problem are we trying to solve, for whom, and what evidence shows that it matters?
  • What outcome and policy intent should the work support, and how will success be measured?
  • What is inside and outside the current scope, and how does it fit into the wider user journey?
  • What relevant services, systems, data, processes and contracts already exist?
  • Which constraints are genuine, what is their source, and how do they affect the options?
  • What important assumptions and risks remain, and how will alpha test or reduce them?
  • Is the likely value of learning more about the problem worth the cost of an alpha?

This is a readiness check, not a mandatory document or a claim that uncertainty has disappeared. Record the answers at the level of detail justified by the work. A concise problem brief may be enough for a low-risk change; a sensitive, costly or cross-government service will need stronger evidence and broader assurance.

You understand enough to explore solutions when you can explain the problem, evidence and uncertainty without relying on the name of a technology.

This is one practitioner's account of how a Solution Architect contributes to problem framing and discovery in UK central government. It does not replace the Service Manual, departmental governance or the responsibilities of the wider multidisciplinary team.
