What a Solution Architect actually does

Most people learn the role by doing it, without a clear picture of what it actually demands. This guide gives you the honest, practical version.

This guide covers:

  • what a Solution Architect does in practice
  • how architectural decisions should be made
  • how the role works with other architecture disciplines
  • what changes when you work in UK government

The role of a Solution Architect

A Solution Architect turns a problem into a solution that can be delivered, operated and changed. That means understanding what the organisation is trying to achieve, exploring different ways of achieving it, and helping people make informed technical decisions.

The Government Digital and Data Profession Capability Framework describes a Solution Architect as someone who designs solutions for problems that affect an organisation. The role includes helping to define the problem and desired outcomes, understanding constraints, developing solution options, making or guiding architectural decisions, documenting designs and working with governance and assurance.

In practice, this puts the Solution Architect between organisational needs and technical delivery. You need enough understanding of both to see how decisions in one area affect another.

The solution is wider than the technology. It may need to satisfy user needs, policy, legislation, security requirements, data considerations, commercial constraints, operational support and existing organisational standards. The architecture has to bring those concerns together into something that can realistically be delivered.

The emphasis changes during the lifecycle. In discovery you may spend most of your time understanding the problem and constraints. During alpha you may compare approaches and test assumptions. During delivery you are more likely to resolve design questions, manage architectural risks and respond to what the team learns. Existing services bring another set of questions around reliability, technical debt, cost and change.

A typical week might therefore include:

  • working with policy or business colleagues to understand a requirement
  • comparing possible solution approaches and their trade-offs
  • reviewing an integration, data or security design with specialists
  • recording an architectural decision and the evidence behind it
  • explaining a recommendation to technical and non-technical stakeholders
  • reviewing a supplier proposal or a change to an existing design

Producing diagrams and documents is part of the job, but they are evidence of the architecture work rather than the purpose of it.

Architecture is a decision-making process

A common mistake is to start with a requirement and jump immediately to a technology. Architecture should create enough space to understand the problem and compare reasonable alternatives.

A useful sequence is:

  1. Understand the problem. What outcome is needed, who needs it and what problem are you actually trying to solve?
  2. Identify requirements and constraints. These might include user needs, policy, legislation, security, existing systems, delivery deadlines, skills, budget or contractual commitments.
  3. Define the evaluation criteria. Decide what matters when comparing approaches. Cost, delivery risk, interoperability, operability, accessibility, security and ability to change may all matter, but their importance depends on the problem.
  4. Develop credible options. Include alternatives that genuinely could solve the problem rather than creating several variations of an option you have already chosen.
  5. Compare the trade-offs. An option may be cheaper but harder to change, quicker to implement but increase dependency on a supplier, or operationally simpler while offering less control.
  6. Make or recommend the decision. Record the reasoning, evidence, assumptions, risks and consequences at a level proportionate to the importance of the decision.

Not every technical decision belongs to the Solution Architect. Developers, technical architects, security specialists, data specialists, product teams and others will make decisions within their areas. Higher-risk or cross-cutting decisions may also need formal governance or approval.

The architect's job is to make sure important architectural decisions are taken at the right level, with the relevant people involved and with enough evidence to explain why the chosen approach was reasonable.

How the role works with other architects

Architecture roles overlap, and organisations divide responsibilities differently. The Government Digital and Data Profession Capability Framework nevertheless provides useful distinctions.

Enterprise Architects work across the organisation. They connect business strategy with change and technical delivery, and develop enterprise architecture visions, strategies, roadmaps, principles and standards.

Technical Architects provide technical leadership and architectural design. They often work closer to implementation and help teams resolve detailed technical issues, apply patterns and ensure technical designs fit the wider strategy.

Business Architects develop integrated views of an organisation or business area and use them to help implement strategy. Their work can include capabilities, organisational structures, processes and relationships between different parts of the business.

Data Architects shape how an organisation designs and manages data so that it supports organisational needs. Their work can include data models, metadata, standards and the relationship between data and wider system design.

The Solution Architect works across these concerns for a particular solution or problem. You may contribute to each area, but specialist architects can provide deeper expertise where the complexity or risk warrants it.

On a small team one person may cover several of these responsibilities. The job titles matter less than making sure important architectural concerns have an appropriate owner.

Four useful ways to think about the work

There is no official set of "four hats" for Solution Architects, but the following model is a useful way to think about the different kinds of work involved.

Translator

Architecture brings together people who describe the same problem differently. Policy may focus on outcomes and legislation, security on threats and controls, commercial teams on contracts, and engineers on implementation.

The architect needs to translate between those perspectives without removing the detail that matters.

Decision maker and adviser

Architects make some decisions themselves and advise on others. The important part is understanding who has authority to make the decision, what evidence is needed and who will be affected by it.

Risk manager

Every option introduces risk. A new technology may create skills or maturity risks. An established platform may constrain future change. Building software creates maintenance responsibilities, while buying a product introduces commercial and supplier dependencies.

The aim is not to eliminate all risk. It is to understand the material risks, reduce them where sensible and make sure remaining risks are visible to the people responsible for accepting them.

Design and assurance

The architect also helps the team check that the design continues to meet its requirements and constraints as it evolves. That can involve security, resilience, integration, performance, accessibility, supportability, cost and other quality concerns relevant to the service.

Assurance should be proportionate. A low-risk internal tool should not automatically receive the same architectural process as a high-volume service handling sensitive information.

What changes in UK government

Government architecture has additional standards, policies and assurance processes that affect technology decisions. Which ones apply depends on the organisation, service and type of change.

Technology Code of Practice

The Technology Code of Practice contains 13 points for designing, building and buying government technology. They cover areas including user needs, accessibility, openness, open standards, cloud, security, privacy, reuse, integration, data, commercial strategy, sustainability and the Service Standard.

The useful architecture question is not simply whether a design can be labelled "compliant". Consider how each relevant point affects the options you are evaluating and record important exceptions or constraints.

Service Standard

The GOV.UK Service Standard contains 14 points covering how government services should be designed and operated.

For central government, transactional services must normally have a service assessment even when the service is internal and used only by civil servants. Other services can still use the Standard, and organisations may have their own assurance arrangements.

Architecture contributes to several parts of the Standard, including security and privacy, technology choices, open standards and common components, and reliable operation. Meeting it is a multidisciplinary responsibility rather than an architecture task alone.

Cloud and common government platforms

Central government's Cloud First policy means cloud solutions should be evaluated before other hosting options. It does not remove the need to assess security, cost, operational capability, legacy dependencies, lock-in and other requirements. Where another hosting model is chosen, the reasoning should be evidence-based.

Government teams should also consider existing platforms and reusable components before creating alternatives. GOV.UK Notify, GOV.UK Pay, GOV.UK Forms and GOV.UK One Login are examples, but applicability depends on the service. In particular, current government guidance requires applicable public-facing services that need users to sign in and prove their identity to use GOV.UK One Login, subject to the stated scope and exceptions.

Other constraints

Depending on the service, architecture decisions may also be affected by data protection, security requirements, accessibility obligations, commercial arrangements, records management, sustainability requirements and organisational technology standards.

Welsh-language requirements may also apply to organisations and services operating in Wales. The exact obligation depends on the organisation and the standards that apply to it, so this should be established rather than assumed.

These are inputs to the architecture decision. They should help define the requirements and evaluation criteria rather than being checked only after a technology has been selected.

Common misconceptions

"A Solution Architect is a senior developer who draws diagrams."

Technical experience can be valuable, but the role also requires problem definition, option analysis, communication, architectural decision-making and an understanding of the wider organisational context.

"The architect designs the solution and hands it over."

Architecture usually continues through delivery because requirements, risks and technical understanding change. Designs should be reviewed and adapted when new evidence justifies it.

"The architect has the final say on technical decisions."

Authority depends on the organisation and the decision. Teams should make decisions at the lowest appropriate level while escalating decisions whose risk, cost or wider impact requires broader agreement or governance.

"Architecture means choosing technologies."

Technology selection is only one part of the work. Choosing a product or cloud service before understanding the problem, criteria and alternatives is usually a sign that the architecture process has started too late.

"A Solution Architect needs to know everything."

The role requires enough breadth to identify important questions, dependencies and risks. It also requires knowing when specialist expertise is needed. Security, networking, data, accessibility and other areas can become too specialised for one architect to cover in sufficient depth.

Questions to ask on a real project

When you are unsure what the Solution Architect should be doing, start with these questions:

  • What problem are we solving and what outcome are we trying to achieve?
  • Who are the users and what evidence do we have about their needs?
  • Which requirements and constraints genuinely affect the solution?
  • What criteria will we use to compare the available approaches?
  • What credible options have we considered?
  • What do we gain and give up with each option?
  • What risks and assumptions could change the decision?
  • Who has authority to make or approve the decision?
  • What evidence should we record so someone can understand the reasoning later?
  • What would cause us to revisit the decision?

If you can answer those questions clearly, the diagrams, architecture documents and decision records become much easier to produce because they are describing reasoning that has already taken place.

This is one practising architect's account of the role in UK central government, not official guidance. Local government and the NHS work differently.
