Architecture decision records in practice

Every architecture is the sum of its decisions. This guide shows you how to capture them so the reasoning outlives the people who made it.

You'll come away with:

  • What an ADR captures and why twenty minutes of writing saves weeks of confusion later
  • The structure of a good record and how to present options honestly
  • Where the line sits between decisions that warrant an ADR and those that do not
  • How your ADR set supports spend controls, service assessments and audit

What an ADR is and why it matters

An Architecture Decision Record is a short document that captures a single significant architecture decision. Michael Nygard popularised the idea in 2011, and it has spread across UK government since, for a simple reason. It fixes a problem every programme has.

The problem is memory. Government programmes run for years and staff rotate every eighteen months to two years on average. The decisions that shape a system, the technology choices, the integration patterns, the trade offs accepted and the options rejected, usually live only in the heads of the people who made them. When those people move on, and in the civil service they always do, the reasoning leaves with them. New teams inherit systems they do not understand, repeat mistakes that were already made once, and reverse decisions without knowing why they were made in the first place.

An ADR fixes this cheaply. When you make a decision that is significant, hard to reverse, or likely to be questioned later, you write it down in a structured record: the context, the options considered, the decision, and the consequences. Twenty minutes of writing. A dedicated, findable, dated record, rather than a meeting note that gets lost, a Slack message that scrolls away, or a memory that resigns.

The payoff goes beyond institutional memory. Writing the record sharpens the decision itself, because if you cannot articulate in writing why you chose Option A over Option B, you have not thought it through carefully enough. It gives you an audit trail, which matters in government where technology spending faces spend controls, service assessments, internal audit and the occasional National Audit Office review. Treat that as professional accountability rather than paperwork.

It also keeps disagreement professional. When a decision sits in a record with its full context and trade offs, people can challenge it constructively. They can say the context has changed since this was decided, or that a new option exists that did not exist then. Without the record, disagreements about old decisions turn personal, because there is nothing else to argue with.

The reasoning leaves when the people do. An ADR is how you keep it.

The anatomy of a good record

Several templates exist, and most of them work. The one that serves government use best runs to seven sections, and each one earns its place.

The title makes the decision findable. Write "ADR 014: Use PostgreSQL for the applicant data store", never "ADR 014: Database decision". A reader scanning the index should know what was decided without opening a single file. The status tells them whether the decision still stands, and the date matters because context shifts. A choice that was right in 2021 may need revisiting now, and the date is what prompts that question.

Context is where you describe the situation that forced the decision. What problem were you solving, what constraints existed, what had already been decided that shaped your options. Be specific, and include the political and organisational context as well as the technical. In government, decisions are shaped by procurement timelines, spending review cycles, ministerial priorities and cross departmental dependencies. Capture them, because a future reader who does not know about the constraint will not understand the choice.

Options considered lists the alternatives you evaluated, each with its genuine advantages and disadvantages. The decision itself should be one or two clear sentences, with any conditions or caveats stated alongside. Consequences is where you show you thought past the immediate choice: what becomes easier, what becomes harder, what risk you have accepted, and what this decision forces or enables elsewhere.

If you want more, add decision makers, links to related records, and a review date. But those are extras. The seven core sections are the record.

When to write one and when not to

Not every decision deserves a record. Write too many and you dilute their value and create maintenance you will resent. Write too few and the reasoning that mattered is lost. The skill is knowing where the threshold sits, and two questions find it: how much does this decision matter, and how expensive is it to reverse.

The clear cases sit in the top right. Your primary database, your hosting platform, your integration pattern, your authentication approach. These choices shape the system for months or years and reversing them is expensive, so they get a record every time. Beyond that quadrant, a few other signals push a decision over the line:

  • It will be questioned later. If you can predict someone asking why in six months, write the record now while the reasoning is fresh. In government that covers anything likely to surface at a spend control, a service assessment or an audit.
  • There was genuine disagreement. If the team debated it, capture the debate and how it was resolved. That stops the same argument recurring every quarter.
  • It affects other teams. If your choice constrains or enables someone else's work, a record gives them the context without needing you in a meeting.

Equally, know what to leave out. Standard, obvious or trivial choices need no record: using your department's standard pipeline unchanged, or picking between two equivalent JSON libraries. Neither do implementation details. The choice between two ways of structuring a module belongs in a code review, and anything you could reverse in a day of work rarely deserves the ceremony.

A working heuristic: if the decision would appear in a solution architecture document, it probably warrants an ADR. If it would only appear in a code review comment, it probably does not.

Honest context, honest options

The most common failure in ADRs is dishonesty. Rarely deliberate lying, but the unconscious pull towards presenting the chosen option as obviously correct and the rejected ones as obviously flawed. Give in to it and the record becomes worthless, because it no longer tells future readers what the real trade offs were.

Honest context means writing down the real situation, including the uncomfortable parts. If a procurement framework limited your options to one vendor, say so. If you chose the simpler architecture because the team lacked the skills for the sophisticated one, say so. If the timeline forced you into the more expensive route, say so. Future readers need the real constraints, and an idealised version will only mislead them.

Honest options means presenting every alternative fairly. Each option you considered had genuine advantages, or you would not have considered it. State them clearly, including for the options you rejected. And the option you chose has disadvantages, so state those too. A record that presents the winning option as having no downsides is not credible, and assessors notice.

You can tell the difference at a glance. The dishonest version describes the chosen option as modern, scalable and aligned with strategy, then dismisses the rejected ones as legacy, expensive and risky. That is advocacy dressed as analysis, and every rejected option has been set up as a straw man. The honest version reads differently. The managed service brings lower operational overhead and automatic scaling, at the cost of a higher unit price and a dependency on one cloud provider. The self hosted alternative avoids licence costs and lock in, but demands an operations team you do not currently have. The emerging platform fits the use case best on paper, with a thin production track record and no government adoption to point at.

The second version is the useful one. It lets future readers reassess when the context changes, and it shows assessors and auditors that the thinking was rigorous rather than retrofitted.

Give the chosen option no downsides and the record reads as advocacy. Nobody is fooled for long.

The status lifecycle

ADRs are living records. In a programme that runs for years, decisions get made, revisited and replaced, and the status field is how the record set keeps up with reality.

Proposed means the decision has been drafted but not yet agreed. It is useful when you want to circulate a decision for review before committing, and in government, where sign off often spans several stakeholders, it gives people a window to challenge before the decision becomes official. Accepted is the normal active state: the decision is agreed and the system is being built or run according to it.

Deprecated means the decision is still technically in effect but no longer the recommended approach. New work should not follow it, though existing implementations need not change immediately. This state is common in government, where legacy decisions coexist with newer approaches through long transition periods. Superseded means the decision has been replaced, and the record should link to its successor. Never delete a superseded ADR. It is part of the decision history, and it shows how the thinking evolved.

Some teams add Rejected or Under Review. Resist the urge to invent more. The four core statuses cover almost everything, and a simple scheme gets used where an elaborate one gets ignored.

The discipline that makes the lifecycle work is updating status when it changes. A record left in Accepted long after it was superseded confuses everyone who finds it, because they now hold two contradictory active decisions. When a new decision changes an old one, update both records the same day. And expect the set to accumulate superseded entries over a long programme. That is healthy. It means the architecture is responding to changing context rather than frozen in a design that no longer fits.

Storing them where people will find them

Where you store ADRs matters as much as what you write in them. A record nobody can find might as well not exist.

The strongest home is the version control repository alongside the code the decisions relate to. The records get versioned with the code, they surface in pull requests when decisions change, anyone with repository access can find them, and they pass through the same review process as everything else. A typical layout is a decisions directory in the repository root, with files numbered in sequence and named from the title, so the directory listing reads as a chronological index of every choice made.

Programmes with several repositories face a choice, and the answer mirrors how decisions actually work. Keep component specific records in the repository they belong to, and hold a central repository for the cross cutting decisions that shape the whole platform. Some decisions are local, some are global, and the storage should say which is which.

Confluence, SharePoint and other wiki tools are weaker homes. They lack version control, they make it awkward to link a decision to the code change that implemented it, and they drift into disorder over time. But if your team lives in Confluence and will not adopt repository based documentation, Confluence ADRs beat no ADRs. Pragmatism beats purity.

Wherever they live, make them discoverable. A new team member needs to find the relevant records without reading all of them, so keep an index file listing every ADR with its status and a one line summary, and cross reference related records. In government, where staff rotate constantly, the first thing a new architect should do on joining a programme is read that index. It compresses years of decision history into twenty minutes, and it saves weeks of asking questions and guessing at answers.

Where ADRs earn their keep in government

ADRs are worth having anywhere, but the governance and assurance that surrounds government technology gives them particular value. The same set of records serves several audiences at once, and each of them would otherwise cost you days of retrospective writing.

Spend controls require you to show the Central Digital and Data Office that you considered alternatives, chose rationally, and understood the implications. A set of well written ADRs is exactly that evidence. Rather than drafting a retrospective justification for the panel, you point at decisions captured in real time with their full context, which is more credible and far less effort.

Service assessments look for the same thing through a different lens. Assessors want evidence that options were weighed, trade offs understood, and choices aligned with the Technology Code of Practice. Your ADRs are the most direct evidence you can hand them, and an assessor who can read your reasoning will trust your architecture far more than one who has to extract it by questioning.

Internal audit and the National Audit Office turn up occasionally, usually on programmes that have overspent or underdelivered. When they do, a clear decision trail is worth a great deal. The records show that choices were made deliberately, with proper consideration, at identifiable points in time. The alternative is reconstructing history from memory and old meeting notes, months or years later, and it is as unreliable as it sounds.

There is a defensive value too. Government decisions get challenged politically. A minister asks why that technology was chosen, a select committee questions the cost, a Freedom of Information request demands the rationale. An ADR gives you a professional answer written at the time of the decision rather than assembled under pressure after the fact. Treat the habit as protection for you, your team and your programme, because the twenty minutes you spend writing a record today can save you days of justification later.

How the practice goes wrong

Having argued for ADRs, it is only fair to be honest about how they fail.

The commonest failure is length. A record should run to one or two pages, and if yours is longer you are packing too much into a single decision. Split it, or move the detailed analysis into a separate options paper the record references. The second failure is timing. A retrospective ADR beats nothing, but it loses the honesty of capture in the moment. Write one weeks after the event and you rationalise without meaning to: the options you briefly weighed and dropped disappear, and the outcome reads as more inevitable than it ever was. Write at the time of the decision, or as close to it as you can manage.

"An ADR is a form you fill in to get a decision approved."

This misunderstanding kills the practice faster than anything else. An ADR records a decision. It does not authorise one, and it is not a change request awaiting six signatures. If your process demands formal approval from multiple boards before a decision can even be written down, you have turned a lightweight habit into heavyweight governance, and teams will quietly stop doing it.

Two more failures are quieter but just as corrosive. Stale status, where superseded records still read as Accepted, leaves new joiners holding contradictory decisions and wondering which to trust. Make the status update part of making the new decision. And inconsistent adoption undermines the whole set: if only some decisions get records, nobody can rely on the collection being complete, and they are back to asking around. A consistent set of plain records is worth more than an occasional brilliant one.

Which points to the right way to start. Start light. Title, status, context, decision, consequences, in a markdown file, is enough. Add options considered, decision makers and review dates as the habit matures. If writing a record takes more than half an hour, something is wrong: either the decision is too big for one record and needs splitting, or the format has grown heavier than the thinking it captures.

A simple record written every time beats a perfect template nobody uses.
