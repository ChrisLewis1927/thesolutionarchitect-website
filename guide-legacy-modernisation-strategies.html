Legacy modernisation strategies: how to choose and how to deliver

Choose a legacy modernisation approach and plan a controlled transition.

You'll come away with:

  • How to assess legacy status and prioritise risk
  • How to choose between six practical modernisation strategies
  • How to plan incremental replacement, data migration and recovery
  • How to assign ownership, maintain the replacement and measure results

New to modernisation? The first three sections cover choosing an approach and moving across safely. The rest cover people, contracts, funding and measurement. Come back to them when you're preparing a business case.

What makes a system legacy

Age doesn't tell you whether a system needs modernising. What matters is whether it still meets users' needs, can be supported and secured, and can be maintained at an acceptable cost.

Take a licensing service. Its database is approaching the end of support. Partner organisations want an API. Staff find it hard to change the rules for licence applications. All three problems sit in one service, but each may need a different response. This guide follows that service throughout.

The government's August 2026 definition of legacy IT is the basis for the assessment. It centres on persistent, unacceptable burden or risk, and an inability to support or secure a system effectively or economically. Systems are classified as Accepted, Action-Required or Legacy. Action-Required means the system will become legacy within 12 months without action. Legacy means it needs urgent remediation.

To classify a system, work through the thresholds for seven criteria with the risk owner, the person accountable for managing the risk: support, contracts, skills, business needs, hardware, cyber security and legacy dependencies. Missing tests or documentation show you where to look, but don't on their own make a system legacy.

The Legacy IT Risk Assessment Framework then helps you prioritise. The score ranks risk; it doesn't decide legacy status. Keep the evidence behind both: support dates, incidents, vulnerabilities, skills, costs and dependencies. A low combined score can hide one serious issue, so every assessment should end with an agreed action, an owner and a review date. How the framework scores is in the Reference section at the end.

The six strategies and how to choose

Start with the outcome you need. Removing unsupported software, enabling a policy change and reducing running costs lead to different choices, even for the same system.

The six strategies below are a teaching model, not an official or exhaustive list. Other models, such as AWS's migration strategies, draw the lines differently. You can combine several in one plan.

Strategy What it means Consider it when Watch for
Retain and manage Keep the system running, with support, monitoring, recovery and the knowledge to maintain it The risk is acceptable, or you need a holding step while a replacement is prepared Any unacceptable risk still needs treatment, even if keeping the system looks cheaper
Wrap and extend Add an API or adapter so other services can use existing functions Others need access to what the system already does, like the licensing partners The core's support, security and change constraints remain. The new interface needs access controls, capacity and failure handling
Replatform Move to a different platform with limited application changes, such as a managed database The platform is the problem: support ending or a heavy operating burden Application design problems stay. Moving unchanged software is rehosting. Modernising doesn't require a move to the cloud
Refactor or re-architect Improve the internal structure, or change the boundaries between parts of the application Business rules are hard to change, or one function needs its own release cycle In software engineering, refactoring keeps observable behaviour the same. Migration guides sometimes use the word more loosely
Replace Build something new or adopt a product, including software as a service Parts of the application hold the service back, such as licence rules that are hard to change Fit to needs, integration, moving data in and out, running costs and supplier dependency. Buying still means data migration, training and process change. It can happen in stages
Retire Switch the system off Its function is no longer needed or has moved elsewhere Confirm nothing depends on it. Put records retention, archive access and disposal in place first

For the licensing service, an API wrapper would meet the partners' need but leave the database deadline in place. Upgrading, replatforming or replacing the database deals with the deadline. If the application makes licence rules hard to change, that needs work too. A phased plan might fix the support deadline first, add the partner API, then replace the parts of the application that hold back policy changes. A real service would sequence these by its own risks and dependencies.

Deadlines, service continuity, security and data protection, skills, contracts and funding all shape what's feasible. Compare credible options against agreed criteria, with today's arrangements, costs and risks as the baseline. Cost the whole transition: discovery, data cleansing, integration, running two systems, training, commercial exit and decommissioning. If one assumption could change the decision, test it with a small experiment before committing to a larger programme.

The least disruptive option deserves a look, but it may keep the underlying problem or cost more over time. Show that trade-off in your recommendation, with the evidence, assumptions and what would trigger a review.

Develop the options with operational, security, data and commercial colleagues. Record who owns the service outcome, who can accept residual risk (the risk left once your changes are made) and who approves the funding, so the recommendation can become an authorised plan.

Moving across in stages: the strangler fig

Replacing everything at once is hard to justify. The strangler fig approach moves functionality across gradually, with the old and new systems running side by side. It's named after a vine that seeds in a tree's upper branches, grows down until it roots in the soil, and over many years strangles the tree it started on.

For the licensing service, the first slice could be the new application journey. Pick a slice with a clear outcome and manageable dependencies. Before starting, understand its business rules, data, batch jobs and downstream consumers, the systems that rely on its outputs. Some dependencies may need changing first.

An API gateway or reverse proxy, a component that sits in front of both systems, can send each request to the right one as slices move. Microsoft's Strangler Fig guidance flags the work around shared data and cross-system dependencies, and the risk of the routing layer becoming a bottleneck or single point of failure. If requests can't be intercepted, or the old system must close quickly, another approach may suit better.

Running both systems needs its own design, funding and support. Before moving each slice, agree four things:

  1. How you'll know it works. Decide which behaviour must stay and which defects or obsolete rules should change. Test representative cases, edge cases and operational performance. Shadow testing, where the new system processes real inputs but its outputs aren't acted on, lets you compare results without triggering real payments or other duplicate actions.
  2. Which system owns the data. Name the authoritative writer, the one system allowed to create or change each record, at every stage. Plan the initial transfer, validation and reconciliation. If changes are synchronised, handle delays, duplicates, ordering and conflicts. If both systems write to the same records, conflict handling must be designed and tested.
  3. How you'll recover from failure. Agree the triggers, who decides and the tested steps. Once the new system accepts writes, switching traffic back may mean replaying data, reconciling records or pausing the service. Schema changes can close the rollback window, the period when switching back is still safe. After that, a forward fix in the new system is usually the safer option. State the expected recovery time and possible data loss.
  4. What counts as complete. Set exit criteria for each slice and keep the remaining old system supported. Plan to remove temporary adapters, synchronisation and old contracts, and fund that temporary architecture in the business case.

The real test for the new licence journey is the whole service. Staff can process applications, downstream systems receive the right information, and recovery works with the data created since cutover.

Retire the old system only when operational and data owners confirm that no users, integrations, scheduled jobs or reports depend on it. Complete the archive, retention, recovery and disposal arrangements before closing licences, infrastructure and support.

The people and contracts behind the system

The people who run and maintain the service know the difficult cases, workarounds and rules that documentation misses. A maintainer may know why an overnight job exists, or how an unusual application is handled. Involve them from the start and work through which rules still serve a purpose.

Give experienced staff clear roles in testing and transition, with time for knowledge transfer and training alongside their live-service work. Explore their concerns rather than assuming resistance to change.

Contracts can limit what's possible. With commercial and legal colleagues, review access to code and data, licence restrictions, intellectual property rights, notice periods, exit charges and transition assistance. The Sourcing Playbook's section on contract end calls for early exit planning, clear responsibilities and enough time for knowledge transfer. Check that against the rights your current contract actually gives you, and agree anything extra you need.

Making the case for investment

Leaders need to see the current constraint, the evidence for it and how the work will improve the service. Useful outcomes include fewer interruptions, supported technology, the ability to make a policy change and lower whole-life cost.

Keep claims specific. Moving the licensing database to a supported platform may reduce support risk without making licence rules any quicker to change. If faster policy change is also promised, show which part of the plan delivers it and how you'll measure it.

HM Treasury's Green Book is the basis for appraising costs, benefits and risks across options. It's mandatory for UK government departments and arm's length bodies (public bodies that work at a distance from ministers) developing proposals involving significant public spending. Work with finance and delivery colleagues on a proportionate business case that includes the cost of transition as well as the end state.

The Digital Assurance Playbook, section 8.7, asks assurers to check how initiatives tackle existing legacy debt or avoid creating future legacy. Use that scrutiny to strengthen the proposal, then follow your organisation's approval and funding arrangements.

If the work is phased, give each stage a useful result and clear criteria for deciding whether to continue. Include the cost of stopping safely or supporting a partly migrated service. A successful first slice is evidence for the next funding decision.

Preventing the next generation of legacy

The replacement needs care for its whole life, because support dates, dependencies, contracts and skills keep changing. The government's guidance on preventing technical debt and legacy requires a legacy-proofing plan for digital products and services. It asks for a business risk owner, a technical health owner, upgrade funding and an asset register, and requires scoring of associated legacy technology.

For the licensing service, that means naming who monitors the replacement's health and who funds and does the work it needs. Build those responsibilities into how the service runs:

  • Plan lifecycle work. Track support dates, dependencies and upgrade responsibilities, with time and funding to act before support ends.
  • Keep boundaries clear. Reduce unnecessary coupling, the degree to which one part depends on the inner workings of another, and document interfaces. Weigh independent replacement against transaction, performance and operational needs.
  • Understand managed services. Establish which maintenance the provider takes on and which stays with you. Amazon RDS maintenance guidance, for example, covers updates, engine deprecations and maintenance that can need downtime. Plan for those changes and test compatibility.
  • Maintain evidence and knowledge. Keep architecture decisions, interface documentation and runbooks current. Automate repeatable checks and test important business rules and recovery paths, so your evidence goes beyond a test coverage percentage.
  • Plan commercial exit. Agree usable data exports, transition assistance and exit responsibilities before signing, then test that they work.

Measuring whether it's working

Measure against the outcomes agreed at the start. Set a baseline before changing anything, and give each measure a scope, owner, data source and review interval. Four groups are a useful starting point:

  • Technical health: whether components are supported and ready for upgrades, with evidence that important business rules and recovery paths have been tested.
  • Ability to change: how long it takes to turn an agreed business need into a working change. DORA's software delivery metrics (DevOps Research and Assessment) add change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate and deployment rework rate. Change lead time starts at a code commit, so it covers only part of the path from policy decision to delivery.
  • Risk reduction: progress on named risks, exploitable vulnerabilities, recovery and the skills needed to run the service. Reassess classification and scores using the same scope and criteria.
  • Service outcomes: availability, processing accuracy, user completion, new capabilities and whole-life cost, including the temporary cost of running both systems.

Use the same evidence for delivery teams, programme boards and investment decisions, at the detail each needs, and flag any change in scope or method so comparisons stay meaningful. A lower risk score should prompt a discussion of which risks fell and why. It can't establish every benefit or a precise percentage reduction in risk.

Moving the licensing service off its unsupported database could improve technical health while the time to change licence rules stays the same. That meets a support-risk objective. If faster policy change was also promised, the flat delivery time tells you to look further: at the application, approvals, testing, skills or work queues. Don't assume another technology change will fix it.

End each review with a decision to continue, adjust, pause or close the work. Confirm which dependencies and costs have gone, and name the owners of the risks that remain. That shows when the modernisation has done its job.

Reference

How the risk framework scores

The Legacy IT Risk Assessment Framework scores seven likelihood criteria from 1 to 6 and six impact criteria from 1 to 5, over an assumed three-year period. For likelihood and impact separately, average the mean criterion score and the maximum criterion score, then multiply the two results:

Score = ((mean likelihood + maximum likelihood) ÷ 2) × ((mean impact + maximum impact) ÷ 2)

The maximum is 30, and 16 or above is rated red. Aggregated scores can be fractional. The matrix helps prioritise risk but doesn't determine legacy status.

Currency notes (October 2026)

  • The risk framework is under review to align with the August 2026 definition, which supersedes its older score-based test for identifying legacy. Confirm your organisation's current assessment and reporting arrangements before applying it.
  • The framework still carries the CDDO name. CDDO merged into the Government Digital Service in January 2025.
  • The prevention guidance was published in 2024 and still refers to the previous spend-control process. Apply its lifecycle requirements using the current Digital Assurance Playbook and your organisation's approval arrangements, alongside the updated legacy definition and the risk framework's review notice.
