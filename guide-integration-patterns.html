Integration patterns for government services

Integration is where government architecture quietly comes undone, usually because the cost of connecting systems gets underestimated right up to the point where it bites. This guide walks through the patterns, the trade offs, and the failure modes you will actually meet.

You'll come away with:

  • How to choose an integration pattern that fits the context rather than the fashion
  • When synchronous integration helps and when it quietly makes your service fragile
  • How to deal with the legacy systems that government integration always seems to involve
  • How to handle data transformation and errors so failures stay contained

Integration patterns: an overview

The patterns themselves are not complicated. What trips people up is choosing one out of habit rather than on the evidence in front of them. Point to point feels sensible when you have two systems to join, and it stays sensible right up until you have eight, at which point the wiring diagram looks like a plate of spaghetti and nobody can change anything without breaking something else. A hub tidies that up by giving everything a single place to connect, but it comes with a quieter cost that rarely makes it into the design document: whoever owns the hub owns everyone else's ability to integrate, which in government is a political fact as much as a technical one.

APIs give you a clear, on demand interface and suit request and response work, which is why they dominate modern government services and why the Technology Code of Practice keeps pointing at open standards. Messaging decouples sender from receiver so each can run at its own speed. Events loosen things further still: a system announces that something happened, and anything that cares reacts, without the publisher knowing or caring who is listening. Each step towards looser coupling earns its keep as the estate grows, and each one adds infrastructure you then have to run and a team that has to understand it. Start with the interaction you actually need, not the pattern you would most like to put on your CV.

Start with the interaction you need and the coupling you can afford. The right pattern falls out of that, not the other way round.

Synchronous and asynchronous: choosing the model

This is the decision people get wrong most often, usually because synchronous feels simpler and simpler feels safer. It is simpler, right until the system you are calling slows down or falls over, at which point your service inherits its problems. Call a back office system that has been running for thirty years and let it have a bad morning, and now your users are having one too.

Asynchronous breaks that chain. You accept the request, hand back a reference number, and do the awkward work out of sight. The price is real. You now have to think about messages that fail to arrive, arrive twice, or turn up in the wrong order, and you have to design the screen that says we will update you shortly without making anyone anxious. As a working rule, stay synchronous when the user genuinely needs an answer before they can proceed, an eligibility check or a payment confirmation, and the downstream system is fast and dependable. Go asynchronous when the processing takes time, the downstream system has moods, or your service needs to stay up when its dependencies are not.

Most government services that work well are quietly hybrid. They are synchronous at the front door, where someone is waiting, and asynchronous in the back office, where the slow and unreliable integrations live. A grant service accepts the application and confirms it in seconds, then validates against Companies House, checks for duplicates, and routes to a caseworker in its own time. The user sees a reference number and a promise of an update within five working days, and never sees any of the machinery behind it.

Government platforms and common components

Reaching for a common component before building your own is the right instinct, and it is also what the Technology Code of Practice expects of you. Notify handles email, text messages and letters, including the government specific edges like Welsh language and accessibility. Pay takes card payments and carries the PCI DSS burden so you do not have to. One Login handles identity for citizen facing services, and Forms can replace custom form development entirely for simple data collection. Beyond the platforms, there are cross government APIs worth knowing: Companies House for company data, HMRC for tax data with the right authorisation, Land Registry for property, Ordnance Survey for geography. Each has its own authentication, its own rate limits, and its own data quality quirks.

What the adoption pages tend to skip over is that every component you reuse becomes a dependency you now carry. If Notify has an outage, your service cannot send notifications, and no amount of clever code on your side changes that. Treat these as external systems with their own availability and their own edges. Queue your notifications and retry rather than assuming delivery. Read the service level commitments and set your own availability targets accordingly. And find the boundaries early: Pay handles the common payment flows well and then stops short of the unusual ones, split payments, recurring payments, conditional refunds. You want to discover that during design, when changing your mind is cheap, rather than at a service assessment, when it is not.

Legacy integration challenges

In government, integrating with something old is most of the job. There will be a system that predates your team, has no API, sends a file once a night, and behaves in ways nobody still employed can fully explain. Data access might mean direct database queries that bypass the business logic, overnight file exports, or screen scraping that breaks whenever someone adjusts a field. Sometimes the protocol is proprietary and the client library only runs on a platform you retired years ago.

"The interface is documented, so the integration will be straightforward."

The documentation describes the system somebody intended to build. The running system has twenty years of undocumented validation rules, error codes that mean one thing on Tuesdays and something else on Wednesdays, and edge cases nobody remembers implementing. You will not find these on paper. You will find them in integration testing, three weeks before go live.

The practical move is to stop fighting this and contain it. Put an adapter between your service and the old world so all the strangeness lives in one component you own and can test, and your new service only ever sees clean, modern data. The literature offers variations on the theme. An anti corruption layer translates at the boundary. A strangler fig routes requests through a new system that delegates to the old one until, feature by feature, there is nothing left to delegate. Scheduled data synchronisation avoids touching the old system in real time at all, if a day of latency is acceptable. Which one you pick matters less than the principle behind them all: the old system's quirks are a design input, and your job is to stop them leaking into everything else.

Treat the old system's quirks as a design input, not a nasty surprise, and keep them behind the adapter where they belong.

Data transformation and mapping

Connecting two systems is the straightforward part. Getting them to agree on what the data means is the part that quietly consumes your schedule. One system calls a person an applicant, the next calls them a claimant, a third calls them a customer, and somewhere a fourth has decided gender is recorded as one or two with no surviving note of which is which. One system holds an address as a single text field while another wants it in four parts, so now you are parsing free text and handling every address that refuses to fit. Dates are the classic trap. They look trivial, then a time zone or a daylight saving boundary corrupts a batch and you lose a day working out why.

Two disciplines save you. Keep all the mapping in one place at the boundary of your service rather than smeared through the code, so it can be found, tested and versioned when an external format changes, and formats always change. And translate everything to a single internal model, so each external system needs exactly one set of mappings instead of one per neighbour. Then test with real data, including the ugly records. Transformation logic that sails through tidy samples tends to fall over the moment it meets production, because production data contains the edge cases that samples were quietly chosen to avoid. When a record still refuses to transform, log it and queue it for a human rather than dropping it, because in government that record is usually a person.

Error handling in integrations

Everything you integrate with will fail at some point, so the useful question is how you respond when it does. A momentary network blip and a permanently rejected record need completely different treatment. Retry the blip with increasing backoff and it resolves itself in seconds. Retry the rejected record forever and you have built a machine for generating load and hiding a problem. The standard patterns exist for exactly this. A circuit breaker stops you hammering a dependency that is clearly down and degrades gracefully instead. A dead letter queue catches messages that repeated retries cannot save, so they wait for a human instead of blocking everything behind them. Idempotency makes retries safe in the first place, so a message handled twice does not become a duplicate application or a second payment landing in someone's account.

In government the stakes are concrete, because a swallowed failure usually has a citizen on the other end of it. A dropped payment is someone who did not receive money they were owed, and that becomes a complaint, then a case, then a headline. So the rules are short and firm:

  • Never drop data silently. Every failure is logged, alerted, and eventually resolved.
  • Never show a user the words something went wrong and walk away. Tell them what happened and what to do next.
  • Give support staff the tools to investigate and fix the failures that automation cannot.
  • Watch error rates, not just individual errors. A rising rate is the early warning, and it usually arrives before the incident does.

Designing integration architecture for government

Put all of this together and a method appears, and it is reassuringly dull. Map the estate first, because the integration you forget is the one that derails you in month four. Write down every system you exchange data with, what flows, in which direction, how often, through what interface, and who owns it. Classify each one: fast or slow, modern or legacy, inside your department or across a boundary, sensitive or not. The right pattern falls out of the classification rather than out of preference.

Then give the whole lot somewhere to live. A dedicated integration layer handles connection, authentication, transformation, retries, logging and monitoring in one place, so the messiness of the outside world stays out of your core service. Assume each integration will fail and decide now how that failure is spotted, explained and put right. Assume each one will change, because external APIs and data formats always do, and version accordingly. Instrument everything, because integration trouble is usually the first symptom of wider trouble. None of this removes the difficulty of integrating in government. It means you meet that difficulty on purpose, with a plan, rather than discovering it live in production with users watching.

Connecting systems is the easy part to picture. Keeping those connections working while the estate around them keeps shifting is the part that actually defines the architecture.
