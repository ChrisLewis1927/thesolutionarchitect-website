Solution options and option appraisal

Option appraisal is where architecture meets accountability. You're spending public money, and every significant technology decision has to stand up to a fair comparison of the alternatives. This guide covers how to do that comparison honestly, in a way that survives a programme board, GDS spend control, and occasionally the National Audit Office.

You'll come away with:

  • How to generate genuinely different options instead of strawmen built to flatter a decision you've already made
  • Evaluation criteria and weightings agreed before scoring starts, not tuned afterwards
  • A government view of buy, build and configure, and why 'do nothing' deserves real analysis
  • How to present options with integrity, including the weaknesses of the one you recommend

Generating genuine options

The hardest part of option appraisal is producing options that differ from one another in more than name. The scoring and the cost model come later, and they're mechanical by comparison. Everyone carries preferences, and those preferences shape which options reach the page before you've consciously decided anything.

The classic failure is the strawman. A modern cloud based build sits next to "keep the fifteen year old system exactly as it is" and "rewrite the whole thing from scratch", and the answer you wanted all along looks inevitable. A seasoned board member or a GDS assessor spots the pattern in seconds, and once they do they stop trusting the rest of your analysis. Worse, if strawmen were the only alternatives you considered, a genuinely better answer may never have made the page at all.

The protection is to start from the problem rather than the solution. "We need a microservices architecture" produces variations on microservices. "We need to process 30,000 grant applications a year with a team of eight" produces approaches that actually differ.

Force yourself to develop at least one option you don't instinctively like. Research it properly and find its real strengths. That discipline keeps confirmation bias in check, and now and then it reveals that your instinct was wrong. Ask the delivery team, the operations team and the business what options they see; they'll suggest approaches informed by perspectives you don't have. Three to five options is usually right. Fewer than three offers no real choice, and more than five dilutes the analysis and wears the board out.

A strawman tells the board you'd rather win the argument than find the right answer.

Defining evaluation criteria

Criteria are the dimensions you compare options against, and the most important thing about them is timing. Agree them, and their weightings, before anyone scores anything. Criteria chosen after the fact and tuned to favour a preferred option are among the easiest forms of dishonesty to commit and among the easiest to detect.

The standard set for government architecture work covers:

  • strategic alignment with the Technology Code of Practice and departmental strategy
  • how well the option serves the identified user needs
  • total cost of ownership across the expected service lifetime
  • delivery risk, including team capability and procurement timelines
  • operational sustainability with the skills the support team actually has
  • security, compliance and the path to accreditation
  • flexibility when policy or demand changes, which in government it will
  • vendor and technology risk over a service life measured in decades

They don't all matter equally. A service with a fixed ministerial deadline weights delivery risk heavily. One handling sensitive data lets security dominate. A service run by a three person operations team can't afford to treat sustainability as an afterthought. When stakeholders disagree about the weights, resist the urge to smooth it over. The disagreement is telling you something real about competing priorities, and it's far better surfaced now than discovered at assessment.

Scoring and comparing options

Scoring is where appraisal feels objective and quietly stops being so. A weighted numerical model that produces 3.7 against 3.5 looks decisive, but a gap that size is almost always noise dressed as signal. Traffic light scoring is usually more honest for government decisions. Red, amber and green tell a board exactly where an option fails, rather than burying the failure inside an average.

Reserve numbers for the things that genuinely quantify, such as cost and performance targets, and use narrative to explain the rest. Whatever method you choose, the biases are predictable. You'll anchor on the first option you assess, quietly inflate the one you prefer, let one strong result spread across unrelated criteria, and favour whatever builds on money already spent. The cheapest defence is a second pair of eyes. Have someone score the options independently, compare where you diverge, and talk through why.

Buy, build or configure in government

Whether to buy a commercial product, build something custom or configure an existing platform is one of the most consequential calls you'll make, and in government it carries dimensions the private sector never faces. The Technology Code of Practice says share and reuse before you buy, and buy before you build, so choosing custom build when a suitable product already exists is hard to defend at spend control.

The useful first question is whether the capability is a commodity or a differentiator. Authentication, notifications and payments are commodities: reach for GOV.UK One Login, GOV.UK Notify or an existing pattern rather than writing your own. A capability that implements unique policy is different, and that's where custom build earns its place. The second question is fit. A product that matches nine tenths of the requirement is worth buying or configuring. Below about seven tenths, the cost of bending it to shape can exceed the cost of building.

Two further questions round the decision out. What can the team actually do? A team without development skills has no business committing to a large custom build, however attractive it looks on paper. And what's the exit strategy? If you buy, you need a route out for when the vendor fails or the price triples. If you build, the code has to be maintainable by a team that isn't the one that wrote it. Watch low code platforms especially carefully. They deploy fast and demo well, and the questions that decide whether you made a good choice, about data portability, maintainability and lock in, only get answered years later.

The 'do nothing' option

Every appraisal needs a 'do nothing' or 'do minimum' option, and it earns its place as the baseline every other option is measured against. Sometimes it's also the honest answer. Including it forces you to articulate the case for change, and if you can't explain clearly why standing still is inadequate, you don't yet have a case strong enough to spend public money on. "The current system works but could be better" will not carry a £5m programme.

The cost of doing nothing is rarely zero. There's maintenance, support, licensing, and the hidden cost of every workaround people have built to cope. Understanding that cost gives you the baseline that makes every other option's price meaningful.

Separate 'do nothing', which means genuinely no change, from 'do minimum', which means the smallest set of changes that removes the most pressing risks: patching vulnerabilities, adding basic monitoring, fixing the worst of the data quality. Do minimum is often the most proportionate option on the table. It handles the immediate risks without committing to a large programme, it buys time for a more considered decision, and it frequently serves value for money best when the case for a bigger investment isn't yet proven. A board will respect you more for putting it there honestly than for pretending the only real choice is a large new build.

If 'do nothing' looks acceptable in your appraisal, take the hint. The case for change needs more work.

Presenting options honestly

How you present options matters as much as how you evaluated them, because a board can only decide well from what you actually show them. Set the context first; don't assume anyone remembers the detail from the last meeting. Give the whole picture at a high level before the detail, then work through each option with what it involves, how it scored, its strengths, its weaknesses, and a cost expressed as a range rather than a falsely precise figure.

When you reach your recommendation, state it plainly and name the trade off you're accepting: "we recommend Option B because it balances cost and delivery risk best, accepting that it scores lower on flexibility than Option C". Then do the thing most architects are tempted to skip and set out the risks of the option you're recommending, alongside the mitigations you propose. A board that discovers those risks later, on its own, stops trusting your judgment.

Separate facts from opinions while you do it. "Option A uses a technology that reaches end of life in two years" is a fact. "Option A is the best choice" is professional judgment. Present the facts first, then the judgment, and respect the board's right to choose differently. If they pick an option you didn't recommend, support it professionally and keep the risks you flagged on the record.

Cost, benefit and risk analysis

Cost benefit risk analysis is the standard frame for appraisal in government. It sits alongside HM Treasury's Green Book, and programme boards and spend control teams will expect to see it. Cost means total cost of ownership across the service's life, typically five to ten years: implementation, migration, hosting and licensing, support, the cost of likely change, and decommissioning at the end. Present it as a range and say plainly what's in and what's out. An early estimate carrying 50% uncertainty is more honest stated that way than dressed up as a precise number.

Benefits won't all reduce to cash, and forcing them to helps nobody. Cash savings attract the most scrutiny. Efficiency gains can usually be expressed as time saved. Risk reduction and strategic value often have to be made qualitatively, with as much specificity as you can manage. Then pair every risk with a credible mitigation. A risk on its own is a problem; a risk with a plan attached is a managed concern a board can accept. The whole analysis should tell one clear story: what the option costs, what it delivers, what it puts at risk, and why that balance beats the alternatives.

Common pitfalls in option appraisal

The arithmetic is rarely what sinks an appraisal. Bias, omission and weak challenge do the damage, and even experienced architects fall into the same traps.

"I already know the right answer. The appraisal just writes it up."

That's the predetermined conclusion, and it's the most damaging trap in this line of work. The criteria get chosen to favour your option, the scoring bends, and the alternatives shrink into strawmen. The antidote is to develop every option properly before you evaluate any of them, so each one has had a fair chance to change your mind.

Close behind it comes the missing option: the genuinely good approach that never made the page because it was unfamiliar, politically awkward, or needed cooperation from another department. Then there's the asymmetry that gathers around a favourite. You estimate its costs more generously because you understand it better, you spot fewer of its risks, and you let sunk investment flatter it because "we've already spent the money", when that money is gone whatever you decide next. False precision rounds out the set: estimates to the nearest pound and scores to two decimal places, projecting a confidence the evidence can't carry.

You can't reliably see these biases in your own work, which is exactly why peer review is the strongest safeguard you have. Hand the appraisal to another architect before it reaches the board, and ask them to find the bias you can't.

Assume the bias is there, invite the challenge early, and treat peer review as part of the work.
